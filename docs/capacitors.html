Drybox Capacitor Retrofit

Two capacitors per PN532, fitted at the module, to stop WiFi transmit current from knocking the I2C bus over.

Why this is the fix

The XIAO and the PN532 share one 3V3 rail. Every time the radio transmits, it pulls hard and fast, and the wiring between the regulator and the PN532 has enough impedance that the module's local voltage dips. Catch a dip mid-byte and the transaction dies, which latches the I2C driver into ESP_ERR_INVALID_STATE — the error filling your serial console.

Here is the budget, from the datasheets. Espressif specifies a supply capable of at least 600 mA for the C5 on its own.

The PN532 is eating the headroom the C5 was specified to have for itself. The capacitors don't add current — they store a small reservoir at the module, so the fast edge is served locally instead of being dragged down the wire.

What capacitors will not fix

They cover transients of tens of microseconds. They cannot hold up a rail that is genuinely sagging for milliseconds — that is an undersized supply, and no sane capacitor value fixes it. If the retrofit doesn't take, suspect the USB cable (charge-only and thin cables are the usual culprits) or the supply itself before reaching for a bigger capacitor.

Does a bigger one help?

Up to a point, yes — and there is a real reason to go past 100 µF rather than superstition. What a capacitor buys you is time: how long it can carry the load before the rail falls too far. Rearranged, C = I · t / ΔV. At the PN532's worst-case 140 mA and a working budget of 100 mV of droop:

Ride out…LastsNeeds
One I²C bit at 100 kHz10 µs14 µF easy
One byte plus its ACK90 µs126 µF the one that matters
A whole PN532 command frame1 ms1400 µF impractical
A 5 ms burst of WiFi TX5 ms7000 µF not happening

Corrupting a single byte is enough to kill the transaction, so the row to design against is the 90 µs one — and 100 µF sits right on that edge with no margin at all. Going to 220 µF or 470 µF buys genuine headroom and is the upgrade worth making.

Past that it stops paying. Nothing in the thousands of µF is realistic, and going much bigger brings two costs of its own: a large bank looks like a short at switch-on, and enough of it will make a current-limited USB supply fold back — browning the board out at boot, which is the very fault you are trying to cure. Bigger cans also have higher parasitic inductance, so they are slower on exactly the fast edges the job is about.

Better than bigger: a mid-range ceramic

At these timescales impedance is set by ESR and inductance more than by value, and ceramics beat electrolytics on both by a wide margin. Adding a 10 µF or 22 µF X7R alongside is the single most effective thing you can do — more so than doubling the electrolytic.

It fills a real gap. The 0.1 µF covers the megahertz end, the electrolytic covers the bulk, and between them sits a band where the impedance of the pair rises. That band is where a transmit burst lives. Bridging it is why the standard stack is three parts and not two: 0.1 µF + 10–22 µF ceramic + 220 µF electrolytic, all across the same two pins. The ceramic is unpolarised, so there is nothing to get backwards.

Reading the marking

Three digits, in picofarads: the first two are the figures, the third is how many zeros follow. So 104 is 0.1 µF and 106 is 10 µF — the two values you want. A trailing letter is tolerance (K ±10%, M ±20%), not capacitance.

The code tells you the value and nothing else — not the dielectric, not the voltage rating, and those are what decide whether the part is any good here. A 106 out of a generic assortment kit is quite likely Y5V or Z5U, because 10 µF in through-hole X7R is comparatively expensive to make. Look for X7R and a 25 V or 50 V rating printed alongside; if neither appears anywhere on the part, assume the worst dielectric and expect to keep only a few µF of it.

A tidy way round that: a surface-mount 1206 or 1210 X7R, 10 µF, 25 V is cheap, unambiguously the right part, and tacks neatly across two adjacent header pins — its end caps land on them almost exactly.

Two things that catch people out with ceramics

Buy a much higher voltage rating than you need. Class II ceramics (X5R, X7R) lose capacitance under DC bias — a 10 µF part rated 6.3 V can be delivering 3–4 µF once it is sitting at 3.3 V, and the datasheet value is measured at zero volts. A 25 V or 50 V part in a larger package keeps most of its value. Avoid Y5V and Z5U outright; their voltage and temperature behaviour is far worse than X7R's.

Don't go all-ceramic. Replacing the electrolytic with a big ceramic looks like an upgrade and can make things worse: a very low-ESR bank and the inductance of your supply wiring form a resonant circuit with nothing to damp it, and the rail can ring rather than settle. The electrolytic's comparatively high ESR is doing useful work. Keep it, and let the ceramic handle the fast end.

If it still browns out, stop adding capacitors

Capacitance covers microseconds. It cannot hold up a rail that is genuinely short of current for milliseconds — that is the supply, and no sane value fixes it. If a box still misbehaves with 470 µF fitted, the fault is upstream: a thin or charge-only USB cable, a weak charger, or a bad connection. A quick check with a meter: measure VCC at the PN532 and 3V3 at the XIAO while it runs. More than a few tens of mV between them is wire resistance, not missing capacitance.

Substitutions that are fine

220 µF or 470 µF instead of 100 µF gives more margin — take it if it fits physically. Low-ESR types are better than standard, but standard works. 6.3 V or 25 V ratings are both fine; 16 V is just the cheapest common part comfortably above a 5 V rail. A general-purpose capacitor assortment kit covers both values in one purchase.

Step-by-step bench detail — pin mirroring, joint quality, iron placement and the short check — is on the PN532 Solder Guide.

Either header — and the free pins are the easy ones

The V3 module carries VCC and GND on both headers: the 4-pin (GND, VCC, SDA, SCL) and the 8-pin (SCK, MISO, MOSI, SS, VCC, GND, IRQ, RSTO). One power net on one small board, so the capacitors work from either.

If your supply wires land on one header, put the capacitors on the other one's unused VCC/GND pins. That is the easier job by a distance: no dupont housing in the way of the iron, no plastic to melt, no reheating a joint that is already carrying the supply, and far less chance of bridging or lifting anything. On the 4-pin header GND and VCC sit next to each other at 2.54 mm, which is exactly the lead pitch to buy.

The electrical cost is a couple of centimetres of PCB trace — on the order of 30 nH against the 150 nH-plus of dupont you are fixing. It is a rounding error on the thing that is actually wrong.

Confirm the two headers really are one net in ten seconds: meter on continuity, one probe on each header's VCC pin, then the same for GND. Both should beep.

Tools

  • Soldering iron with a fine tip, around 320 °C
  • Solder, 0.6–0.8 mm
  • Flush cutters and tweezers
  • A multimeter — for the continuity check in step 6, which is the step that saves you from letting smoke out

Get the polarity right

The ceramic is not polarised and goes in either way round. The electrolytic is, and fitting it backwards makes it vent — loudly, and once.

Fitting one module

  1. Power the box down and unplug it

    USB out at the wall end. The rail you are about to bridge is the one you are trying to fix.

  2. Find VCC and GND on the PN532

    Same header your existing wires come off — the pins are silkscreened. You are fitting both capacitors across this pair, and nothing else on the board is involved.

  3. Trim both capacitors' legs short

    Leave about 8–10 mm. This matters more than it looks: long legs add inductance, which is the exact thing you are fitting the capacitor to defeat. A neat capacitor on long leads is worse than an ugly one sitting flush.

  4. Solder the ceramic across VCC and GND

    Either way round. Underside of the module, directly onto the header pins where they protrude, is ideal — that is as close to the chip as you can get without touching the PCB.

  5. Solder the electrolytic across the same two pins

    Stripe leg to GND, other leg to VCC. Check it before the iron touches anything. Keep the iron on each joint for two or three seconds — electrolytics dislike prolonged heat.

    Check before you power up

    A backwards electrolytic will bulge, vent and stink, and it can take the module with it. It is worth the extra ten seconds of looking.

  6. Test for a short before applying power

    Multimeter on continuity or resistance, probes on VCC and GND. You should see the reading climb as the capacitor charges and settle high. A steady beep or near-zero ohms means a solder bridge — find it before you plug anything in.

  7. Sleeve the legs and power up

    Heat-shrink over any bare leg that could touch something in a vibrating box. Then power on and watch the serial console: you want the banner, then Reader 1 on I2C0 ready, and no 259 errors.

  8. Confirm it with the radio-off test

    In the web UI, Reader diagnostics → Run 5-minute radio-off test. The board reboots, runs with its radio off, then reboots back — so read the verdict off the USB serial console, not off the reset counter, which starts and ends at zero either way. Then leave the box running normally for a working day and watch that counter. Zero is the result you want.

Before you pick up the iron

Two things cost nothing and are worth trying on one box first, if only to see how much of the problem they account for.

  • Turn TX power down. Settings → WiFi TX power → 13 dBm. Applies live, no reboot. Only on a box with signal to spare — check the RSSI reading first.
  • Try 2.4 GHz. Worth 90 mA off every transmit peak, as the chart above shows. You chose the C5 for 5 GHz, so this is a trade rather than a recommendation — but if it stops the dropouts on a box that is far from the AP anyway, it tells you something useful.

Neither is a substitute for the capacitors across eight sealed boxes you would rather not open twice. They are a way to confirm the diagnosis in ten minutes instead of an afternoon.
