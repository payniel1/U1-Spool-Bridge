PN532 Solder Guide

Four joints, two pins, one mistake that costs a module. What the board looks like from the side you will actually be working on.

Before the iron is hot

Both capacitors go across the same two pins — VCC and GND — on the underside of the module, where the header pins come through the board. Nothing else on the module is touched, and the dupont wires stay plugged in on the other side.

Either header — and the free pins are the easy ones

The V3 module carries VCC and GND on both headers: the 4-pin (GND, VCC, SDA, SCL) and the 8-pin (SCK, MISO, MOSI, SS, VCC, GND, IRQ, RSTO). One power net on one small board, so the capacitors work from either.

If your supply wires land on one header, put the capacitors on the other one's unused VCC/GND pins. That is the easier job by a distance: no dupont housing in the way of the iron, no plastic to melt, no reheating a joint that is already carrying the supply, and far less chance of bridging or lifting anything. On the 4-pin header GND and VCC sit next to each other at 2.54 mm, which is exactly the lead pitch to buy.

The electrical cost is a couple of centimetres of PCB trace — on the order of 30 nH against the 150 nH-plus of dupont you are fixing. It is a rounding error on the thing that is actually wrong.

Confirm the two headers really are one net in ten seconds: meter on continuity, one probe on each header's VCC pin, then the same for GND. Both should beep.

Do this to one module first

Fit one box, run it for a day, confirm the reset counter stays at zero, and only then do the other seven. If the pilot still misbehaves the fault is upstream of anything a capacitor fixes — almost always the USB cable or supply — and you will have spent ten minutes rather than an afternoon finding that out.

The mistake that costs a module

You will be soldering on the back of the board, and the pin order reverses when you turn it over. Almost every reversed-capacitor story starts here: the pins were identified from the top, the board was flipped, and the legs went into what were now the wrong two holes.

Three capacitors, two pins

They all go across the same VCC and GND — in parallel is the whole point, and any other arrangement is wrong. But "the same node" and "the same hole" are different things, and the second one will not physically work.

A 2.54 mm plated through-hole takes one component lead and the solder to wet it. Two is a squeeze. Three will crack the plating or lift the pad, and if you widen the hole to make room you have destroyed the barrel that connects the two sides of the board. Pick one of these instead:

ApproachHowBest when
Split across the two headers Electrolytic on one header's free VCC/GND, the two ceramics on the other's. Same net, so still parallel. Your case. Both headers are free of wires on one pair each, and it spreads the mechanical load.
Bundle the legs Twist the two positive leads together and the two negative leads together before soldering, then make one joint per side. Two parts into one pair of holes. Clean and strong.
Stack on the pin Solder the first part, then solder the next part's leg to the first one's leg just above the joint. Adding a capacitor to a module you have already done.
SMD across the pins A 1206 X7R lies flat and bridges two adjacent pins. Uses no hole at all. The 10 µF, and it frees a hole for the others.
Order, if you are being fussy

The 0.1 µF wants to be nearest the module and the bulky electrolytic furthest — smallest and fastest closest to the load is the usual rule. At this scale, with everything within a couple of centimetres on the same board, the difference is small; do not contort the build for it. Mechanical soundness is worth more here than a millimetre of lead length.

These boxes vibrate

Three components hanging off two joints is a fatigue risk in a drybox with a spool rolling around in it. Whatever arrangement you pick, keep the leads short, sleeve anything bare, and anchor the electrolytic's can with a dab of hot glue so its mass is not being carried by the solder joints alone.

What a joint should look like

The whole technique is one idea: heat the metal, then feed solder into the metal. Solder melted on the iron and wiped onto a cold pin looks like a joint and conducts like a rumour.

Where the iron goes

The eight minutes

  1. Unplug the box

    At the wall, not just at the board. The rail you are about to bridge is the one you are trying to fix.

  2. Mark GND on the underside

    Read VCC and GND off the silkscreen on the component side, then dot the GND pad on the back with marker pen. Now turn the board over. This is the step that stops the reversed-electrolytic story.

  3. Trim both capacitors to about 8 mm

    Long legs add inductance, which is the exact thing the capacitor is there to defeat. A tidy capacitor on long leads is worse than an untidy one sitting flush. A 2.5 mm-pitch electrolytic needs no bending at all — its legs land straight on two adjacent header pins.

  4. Ceramic first

    Either way round; it is not polarised. Hook a leg against each pin, heat the pin and the pad together for two or three seconds, feed solder into the far side of the joint, then remove the solder and the iron in that order. Repeat for the second leg.

    The ceramic goes on first because it is the flatter of the two — put the tall electrolytic on first and it stands in the way of the iron.

  5. Electrolytic second — stripe leg to your mark

    The striped side is negative and goes to GND, the pad you dotted. Check it, then check it again, then solder. Two or three seconds per joint: the leads tolerate plenty of heat but the can does not enjoy it.

    Backwards is not a mistake you can undo

    A reversed electrolytic vents — loudly, once — and can take the module with it. Two markings agree so you can check yourself twice: the printed stripe is the negative side, and on an untrimmed part the negative leg is the shorter one. If somebody already trimmed the legs, trust the stripe.

  6. Test for a short — before any power

    Meter on continuity or the lowest resistance range, one probe on VCC and one on GND. You should watch the reading climb as the capacitor charges, then settle high. A steady beep, or a reading that sits near zero and stays there, is a bridge — find it before you plug anything in.

    If the meter beeps briefly and then goes quiet, that is the capacitor charging, not a fault. Reverse the probes and it will do it again.

  7. Sleeve anything bare

    Heat-shrink over any leg that could touch something once the box is closed and moving. A drybox is a vibrating environment with a spool rolling around in it.

  8. Power up and watch the console

    You want the banner, then Reader 1 on I2C0 ready (PN532 fw 1.6), and no 259 errors. Then leave the box running normally for a working day and check the reset counter on the Reader pill. Zero is the result you are after.

If you would rather not solder on the module

Eight modules is eight chances to lift a pad, and a lifted pad on a PN532 is not worth the fifteen minutes saved. There is a reasonable alternative: solder both capacitors onto a short two-way female dupont header instead, and piggyback that onto the module's VCC and GND pins alongside the existing wires.

  • Electrically almost as good — the capacitors still sit a few millimetres from the module, which is what matters.
  • Completely reversible, and you can build all eight on the bench before touching a single drybox.
  • The cost is one more mechanical connection per rail, so seat it firmly and give it a tug before closing the box.

What does not work is putting the capacitors at the other end of the loom, next to the XIAO. That is the end of the wire the problem is already being solved at; the whole point is to have the reservoir at the far end, next to the load.
